What are PFAS?

PFAS, or, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, are long-lasting chemicals that are widely used for consumer, commercial, and industrial purposes, with components that take a long time to break down. Their frequent application and persistence in the environment means that PFAS are found in human and animal blood, as well as in food products and in air, water, and soil.

PFAS have been linked to negative health effects in humans and animals, through numerous scientific studies. Research continues on how much people, animals, and the environment are exposed to PFAS, as well as how to manage and remove these chemicals.

Source: EPA

What is UCMR5?

This study was conducted using data collected from UCMR5 - the Fifth Unregulated Contaminant Monitoring Rule.

Every five years, the EPA issues a list of unregulated contaminants that Public Water Systems (PWSs) should monitor. Between 2023 and 2025, data was collected on 30 chemicals, 29 being PFAS substances. This sampling enables further understanding of how frequently PFAS appear in drinking water systems.

Source: EPA

What are HUCs?

The United States Geological Survey outlines watersheds based on surface hydrologic features. The country is divided into progressively smaller areas with increasingly detailed digit codes (or, HUCs: Hydrologic Unit Codes). The largest subdivision have two-digit codes, while the smallest have 12-digit codes.

This study evaluates PFAS detections at the HUC8 level (subdivisions with 8 digits), as opposed to by city, county, state, or administrative subdivisions.

Our model uses HUC8 regions because predicting PFAS violations based on watershed enables more accurate tracking of PFAS as they travel from industrial sites into waterways and, ultimately, public water systems.

Source: USGS

Project Objective

This website was created in conjunction with a paper titled "Explainable AI Reveals Statistical Associations Between Industrial Activity and PFAS Contamination of Public Water Systems," written by researchers from The University of Texas at Austin.

The project aims to use machine learning to draw correlations between industrial activities, sociodemographics, and likelihood of PFAS detection in the U.S.'s public water systems.

By developing a model to predict risk of PFAS contamination in any given area, we hope to offer a tool that can help people become more aware of how PFAS can appear in the water they use every day.
